The Denials Your Hospital Cannot See

By Christopher Hutchins · November 15, 2026

Featuring Gretchen Heinen · Founder & CEO, Authsnap · on The Signal Room

I remember sitting with stacks of the ones we call EOBs, explanation of benefits, and having to go through them and deal with every line that was an exception. And the worst part of it was knowing that you are probably gonna have to do that two or three times on the same claim before you actually get something processed. It felt like you were getting an immediate denial, and they were gonna deny so many times before they actually would consider it.

This article is drawn from my conversation with Gretchen Heinen of Authsnap on The Signal Room. Listen to the full episode: Denial Management: Hidden Leaks Your Hospital Cannot See.

From the Bedside to the Denial

Gretchen Heinen, a nurse who co-founded Authsnap to take on clinical insurance denials, came on The Signal Room this week. The denials she means are not the clerical kind:

“These are ones where literally the insurance company is coming back and saying, like, I don’t think this patient needed this care. You could have provided lesser care or they didn’t need this drug.”

“I watch patients not be able to get the care that they needed because of a sheet of paper, not because we actually can’t give the care. Like we have the doctors, we have the resources, we have the medicine, we have the technology, but a sheet of paper was preventing their care.”

The Leaks Nobody Can See

Gretchen on why the losses are so hard to catch:

“The insurance companies all have different communication methods to their hospitals and how they handle denied claims and clawbacks. Sometimes they give you a phone call and say this patient isn’t authorized to have this night stay in the hospital. Sometimes they send a spreadsheet with the list of denied claims. Sometimes they will send a remark code documentation where it’s just a list of codes that they decided not to pay.”

“So now imagine a hospital trying to figure out how to create a process for managing their denials when some of it’s coming as a letter in the mail, some of it’s coming as money missing in the bank account, some of it’s coming as a spreadsheet.”

Why the Magic Wand Does Not Work

“Whenever I see companies that are popping up and they’re like, we’re just building AI agents, or we’re an AI company and we’re gonna come in and wave a magic wand and we’re gonna fix your denials. I would love to ask that person, okay, tell me how this is going to happen in this workflow in the hospital. Most technology companies do not understand how patient workflows work.”

I spent a lot of my career on this. I was in a room with a rather large insurance company representative, trying to solve secondary claim processing, and they wanted to sell me a tool to do it. They claimed those things are not together in any system. And I pulled out a paper EOB and I said, nonsense. You got it. It’s right there. Patient gets it. It sure felt like some of that was we are making these systems not talk to each other for a purpose.

What It Costs

“Every week, a physician spends 13 and a half hours on average on things like prior authorization and denials. Imagine if we could take that amount of time off of the physician’s plate. How many more happy physicians would we have? How many less suicides would we have in medicine?”

It seems as though the main focus of the electronic health record was to get more accurate billing. More quality billing. It was never about clinical workflows. But in reality, it sure sounds to me like we have created a whole nother set of issues with denials, and what we did with the right intentions has not been executed properly, and it is still hurting us every single day.

Whose Job This Is

There are people whose job is never going to be to take care of a patient, and a lot of those people are listening to the show. We are the only ones gonna be able to solve this. And we need to make sure that we are not always hoping that somebody else does it, leaning on clinicians and adding more layers.

Who Gets Counted in Health Data?

with Lisa Marceau · Founder & CEO, Alpha Millennial Health

A model can look convincing and still leave people out. Lisa Marceau explains who gets represented in clinical and claims data, why data quality and governance belong at the start, and how to decide whether a product needs AI at all.

Show Notes

A model can look convincing and still leave people out. Lisa Marceau, founder of Alpha Millennial Health and co-founder of Joyuus, joins Chris Hutchins to talk about who gets represented in clinical and claims data, why data quality and governance belong at the start, and what changes when AI is treated as infrastructure instead of innovation. They also cover women's health research, what mothers told Joyuus was missing from postpartum care, and how NIH small business funding helps an idea become a viable company.

What We Cover

  • Who gets represented in clinical and claims data
  • Why data quality and governance belong at the start
  • What mothers told Joyuus was missing from postpartum care
  • How mental, physical, social and real-world health connect
  • Questions leaders should ask before investing in AI
  • Lisa's book, Breaking the System

Key Takeaways

  • Ask the question before you collect the data. Marceau compares product work to research: you set the hypothesis first, then gather data to answer it. That means asking who owns the data, how it will be used, and whether the right data sets exist or have to be created.
  • Missing populations are a data quality problem. She reframes equity and ethics as governance and quality: you cannot have representative data if part of the population is missing, and solving data quality and governance is how you get equity and ethics.
  • Treat AI as infrastructure. Define the problem and the gap first, then ask whether AI makes the work better, faster or more efficient. If it does not, there is no reason to layer it on. She also argues for a human in the loop asking the right questions from the start.

Examples Lisa Used

  • Research studies in which patients with the same cardiovascular symptoms were diagnosed differently, with women more often diagnosed with depression than men
  • The gap between ovarian and prostate cancer research, and how it shows up in how early each cancer is found
  • Joyuus asking mothers and providers what was missing in the 12 months after delivery, and starting with depression as the first use case
  • The NIH small business program as a way to build structure, market understanding and financial discipline alongside a good idea
  • “Ghost data”: fragments of data that may have been deleted but still get pulled into AI answers

These examples reflect Lisa Marceau's perspective and research, offered as leadership insight rather than clinical, legal or funding advice for any specific situation.

Lisa Marceau: The way I talk about data as infrastructure really comes down to this concept of AI as innovation. Where I spend a lot of time talking about this is that AI isn't innovation. It is the foundation that helps us do something better, faster, more efficiently. How are we going to get there? What do we need to do it? Once we answer those questions, we ask, does AI make this better? Does it make it faster? Does it make it more efficient? You're not going to plug into an EMR if you don't need an EMR. You're not going to plug in an AI infrastructure model if you don't need AI to help your product work better.

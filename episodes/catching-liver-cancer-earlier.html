Catching Liver Cancer Earlier

with Dr. Bharat Tewarie · CEO, Helio Genomics

Dr. Bharat Tewarie explains why six-month ultrasound surveillance so often fails the patients who need it most, how the blood-based HelioLiver test detects cancer early enough for curative options, and what the peer-reviewed CLiMB study showed. Show Notes

Liver cancer almost never announces itself, and by the time there is a symptom, the window to cure it has often closed. Dr. Bharat Tewarie, CEO of Helio Genomics, explains why six-month ultrasound surveillance so often fails the patients who need it most, how the blood-based HelioLiver test detects hepatocellular carcinoma early enough for curative options, what the peer-reviewed CLiMB study showed, and why he is pushing to retire one sentence: “we caught it too late.” He joins to translate this for clinicians and patients alike; nothing here is medical advice.

What We Cover

  • Why liver cancer stays silent until it is advanced, and who is at risk (cirrhosis, hepatitis B and C, fatty liver, alcohol, obesity, type 2 diabetes)
  • How current guideline surveillance works (ultrasound plus the AFP blood test every six months) and why follow-through breaks down
  • The access gap: operator-dependent ultrasound, travel burden, and rural America
  • What HelioLiver is: a single blood draw reading methylation on cell-free DNA, protein markers, and clinical factors
  • The CLiMB study: prospective, blinded, MRI-referenced, 1,968 patients, and what it showed versus ultrasound
  • How a result flows through the clinic: a Quest blood draw, EMR integration, and the positive or negative next step
  • What the test is and is not for (patients at risk, not the general population)
  • How the team guards against false positives in an organ that is already damaged
  • Policy: House Resolution 1591, Liver Cancer Awareness Day, and the “coalition of the willing”

Key Takeaways

  • Surveillance fails quietly. Dr. Tewarie cites Medicare data showing only 21% of patients later diagnosed with liver cancer had received even one ultrasound in three years; a blood test done in the same visit is far more likely to actually happen.
  • CLiMB showed a real detection gap. In the peer-reviewed CLiMB study (published May 2026), HelioLiver detected 48% of cancers versus 28% for ultrasound, and for stage 1 the difference was 40% versus 10%, with ultrasound detecting zero lesions under two centimeters.
  • Catch it while cure is still on the table. The goal is detecting cancer early enough for ablation, resection, or transplant; as Tewarie puts it, “every week matters,” and he wants to retire the sentence “we caught it too late.”

Examples Dr. Tewarie Used

  • Medicare patients later diagnosed with liver cancer: only 21% had one ultrasound in three years
  • A positive HelioLiver prompting an MRI after a negative ultrasound, catching a lesion that would have been missed
  • Cases where the test flagged abnormal cell-free DNA up to six months before imaging could see the tumor
  • The rural-health access gap, and a transformation collaborative (co-chaired with Microsoft) working on it

These figures reflect the CLiMB study and Dr. Tewarie's account. HelioLiver is a surveillance test for patients at risk, not a general-population screen, and nothing here is medical advice.

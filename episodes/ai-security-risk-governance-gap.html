AI Security Risk: The Massive Mistake Companies Make

with Aaron Puckett · EVP, Managed Services Group

Most AI-governance conversations happen in the boardroom. Aaron Puckett spends his days one layer beneath it — in the security controls and infrastructure that were built years before anyone said the word “AI.” His argument: the mistake most companies make isn’t writing the wrong AI policy, it’s having nothing underneath it to enforce one.

Show Notes

Most of the AI-governance conversation happens in the boardroom — the policy, the committee, the framework. Aaron Puckett spends his days one layer beneath all of that: the actual infrastructure and security controls that were built years before anyone said the word “AI.” As Executive Vice President at Managed Services Group, a security-first MSP/MSSP running SOC 2 Type 2, HIPAA, and NIST environments for organizations in regulated industries, he argues the mistake most companies make isn’t writing the wrong AI policy — it’s having nothing underneath it to enforce one. He joins Chris Hutchins to name who actually owns the gap between an AI policy and the systems that were never built to govern it.

What We Cover

  • The operator’s seat — what an MSP/MSSP sees that boards and vendors both miss, and why Aaron ties cybersecurity and compliance to risk, operations, and EBITDA instead of treating them as a cost center
  • Policy vs. controls — the gap between what an AI policy says and what an environment can actually enforce, and who inside an organization is supposed to own it
  • Shadow AI and the real risk — frontline staff pasting PHI into public LLMs to move faster, what actually happens to that data, and what stops it when a policy email clearly doesn’t
  • Compliance without margin pain — getting audit-ready for AI on top of SOC 2, HIPAA, and NIST without burning out an already-stretched team, and the highest-leverage move to make first
  • The features you didn’t turn on — how to think about the AI capabilities your existing vendors are quietly switching on inside tools you already trust

Key Takeaways

  • The policy is the easy part. The real question is whether anything underneath it can actually enforce how AI gets used.
  • You cannot ban your way out of shadow AI. Prohibition drives the behavior underground; governance has to assume the tools are already in use and make that use safer.
  • Security and compliance are a business decision, not a cost center. Tied to risk, operations, and EBITDA, AI governance becomes something a board will actually move on.

Frameworks & Ideas Mentioned

  • SOC 2 Type 2, HIPAA, and NIST as the control environment that has to sit underneath any AI policy
  • “Shadow AI” — ungoverned consumer LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) handling sensitive data, including PHI
  • Tying cybersecurity and compliance directly to EBITDA to get board-level movement
  • Resource-aware compliance: the 20% of controls that deliver 80% of the protection for a stretched team

About Aaron Puckett

Aaron Puckett is Executive Vice President at Managed Services Group, a security-first MSP/MSSP that runs SOC 2 Type 2, HIPAA, and NIST environments for organizations in regulated industries. He advises leaders on how cybersecurity, compliance, and AI strategy affect risk, operations, and EBITDA, and completed the University of Central Florida Cyber Defense Professional Program.

▶ Full Episode Transcript

Chris Hutchins: Welcome back to The Signal Room. Most of the AI-governance conversation happens in the boardroom — the policy, the committee, the framework. But there’s a layer underneath all of that: the actual infrastructure, the security controls, the systems that were built years ago, before anyone said the word “AI.” My guest today spends his days in that layer, and he’d argue that’s exactly where governance actually lives or dies. My guest is Aaron Puckett, Executive Vice President at Managed Services Group — a security-first MSP and MSSP that runs SOC 2, HIPAA, and NIST environments for organizations in regulated industries. What I find interesting about Aaron’s lens is that he doesn’t treat security and compliance as a cost center; he ties cybersecurity, compliance, and AI strategy directly to risk, operations, and EBITDA. So he sits in the seat most people never see — the operator’s seat, underneath the policy. Aaron, welcome to The Signal Room.

Aaron Puckett: Well, thank you for having me, Chris. Glad to be here — and you summed it up perfectly.

Chris Hutchins: Before we get into the hard stuff, take a minute and share a little about who you are and what Managed Services Group actually does day to day.

Aaron Puckett: Sure. Managed Services Group is a SOC 2 Type 2, HIPAA-verified, trusted managed security service provider. What does that mean? It means we’ve proven, on an ongoing basis, that we’re audited and that we have the strongest security posture, policies, and procedures. You’ve heard the expression about the cobbler whose kids have no shoes — well, our kids do have shoes. We live what we preach every single day, and we bring that same level to our clients. I’ve been in technology for most of my career — I started with Toshiba right here in Orlando back in 1999, on the business-technology side, and gravitated into managed IT, MSP, and cybersecurity. I went through UCF’s professional cyber-defense program to harden those skills. I’ve dealt with everything from small and mid-sized business to enterprise and government. Managed Services Group is headquartered in Orlando, but we work with heavily regulated industries like finance and healthcare all over the United States.

Chris Hutchins: These terms you listed off — I’ve seen them all over the place, and it’s probably more complicated than we can cover easily. But what makes me feel good is that there are people like you who really understand how to put the right safeguards in to protect data and protect access to it. Oftentimes we’re our own worst enemy — quick to adopt and trust, and then suddenly there’s a whole bunch of information out there that we wish we hadn’t let go. So thank you for what you do.

Aaron Puckett: It’s the age we live in. People unfortunately live their lives the way they do on social media — but with other people’s information, or clients’ information, unknowingly letting it out there without thinking twice about putting it into an LLM.

Chris Hutchins: I told my boys years ago: no one really cares what you’re having for dinner, don’t post that stuff. A little silly, but let’s jump in. You sit in a few different seats, but the operator’s seat is one most of my guests don’t — between the vendors selling the tools and the boards writing the policy. From that seat, what’s the thing about AI risk that both of those groups consistently miss?

Aaron Puckett: They consistently miss that it’s already in their organization. And even if they have a policy, it’s not enforceable — they’re not aware of what tools are actually being used, what information is being leaked, or what those tools have access to. The other misconception is at the board level. Many boards have entrusted their internal IT teams to make those decisions, but it needs to remain in the wheelhouse of the board and the executive leadership team. I’ve seen it delegated to IT teams whose day-to-day is fixing computers or running the help desk — writing company-wide policy really needs to stay with leadership.

Chris Hutchins: Good point. In the healthcare organizations I worked in, we sometimes had this wacky idea that we could build everything ourselves — and what ends up happening is you degrade your operational support and still don’t do a good enough job on the thing you’re building. What would you say to leaders who think they can just layer this onto someone in IT, versus finding the right person with the expertise who’s going to be focused on it?

Aaron Puckett: They need to think hard about entrusting it to someone who sits in IT but may not understand the medical side — the PHI, the PII, the day-to-day operations. It needs to be collaborative: executive leadership, and if they don’t have a CISO or CTO, bring one in to evaluate these AI tools before they’re deployed. And even before evaluation, they need to understand where their data resides, who has access, privileged access, conditional access — all that foundational data hygiene — before they unleash an AI that will just expose every gap that isn’t filled or restricted.

Chris Hutchins: It’s the siloed nature of how data gets managed that concerns me — operational data on one side, financial data on the other, and neither owner thinks the data they put into an AI-connected platform carries risk. By itself, maybe it doesn’t. But if someone can connect operational data to financial data and pull records together, that creates a problem. This isn’t a hobbyist thing or a part-time job — you’ve got to have the right expertise.

Aaron Puckett: That’s where third-party partnerships come in. Just like we had to have a third party to become HIPAA-verified and SOC 2 Type 2 — you can’t self-assess. It’s no different from healthcare: you can do WebMD all you want, but you really need to see a doctor who runs tests, writes a prescription, and gives you a treatment program. That’s what we do — we diagnose the problem, tell you what the issue is, and give you the remedy and remediation. It needs to be looked at exactly that way.

Chris Hutchins: I agree a hundred percent. When a new client comes to you, what’s the gap between what their AI policy says and what their environment can actually enforce?

Aaron Puckett: It’s a little better among financial institutions and healthcare — most have some policy, and some have approved, paid programs. But most have a policy that basically says “use it responsibly,” with no teeth. They have no DLP — data loss prevention — implemented. They haven’t reviewed permission levels: who has access to what. People move from operations to finance to HR — did anyone review their permissions? Do they still have access to PHI, PII, or financial data they shouldn’t? You’ve got oversharing permissions everywhere — everyone shares to anyone who has the link. Where is the information stored? You have APIs, open authentications, single sign-ons — it opens Pandora’s box if you don’t understand how data moves. What I appreciate are the organizations that say, “We understand AI isn’t optional; it’s going to level up my people.” Telling employees they can’t use AI is like saying “don’t use the internet” in 1998 — they’re going to use it whether you like it or not. You need policies, and then you need to govern and monitor them. If it’s just a policy sitting somewhere, there’s no enforceability. Trust your people, but it’s your data — your clients’ and patients’ information — and there’s a lot of value in securing it correctly.

Chris Hutchins: You frame this in terms of EBITDA, which is one of those things that actually makes boards pay attention. Why does that framing matter for getting a board to act?

Aaron Puckett: Many organizations run their risk management on three questions: does it make me money, does it cost me money, and does it mitigate my risk? AI can do all three. It makes you money by making you more operationally efficient — healthcare has a lot of keystrokes and mouse clicks: pulling patient records, referrals, booking appointments, denials. That’s a lot of redundancy and repeatable process. You can level up the people you have without adding headcount, or redeploy people to less administrative work. Take AI transcription: instead of a doctor spending most of the visit typing, he can be engaged with the patient while AI transcribes in the background. But then comes the human in the loop — the doctor has to verify everything that went into the record is accurate, because if it hallucinates, that falls on the doctor and the organization. Done right, that one tool improves quality and continuity of care and lets you see more patients. That’s pulling the levers of EBITDA. You can help prevent fraud and submit and appeal claims faster. But if someone without the proper tools grabs all that information and drops it into a free version of ChatGPT to help with a denial, they’ve just dumped PHI into an unapproved LLM — a HIPAA violation or a potential leak. They thought they were speeding things up, but they used it inappropriately and did damage. Most of it, though, is levers of EBITDA — amplifying the team you have, back office or clinician, and improving patient care.

Chris Hutchins: You mentioned shifting the burden from taking notes to reviewing after the fact. I’ve heard from clinicians that the net impact is just changing how they spend the same amount of time — they want some time back. So for those designing solutions: pay attention, do better. These folks have been under the gun a long time. Let’s talk about the gap between policy and controls. AI governance is showing up after the infrastructure was already built — nobody designed these systems expecting an employee to paste data into a model that learns from it. So when an organization hands you an AI policy, how often is there nothing underneath it?

Aaron Puckett: Many times there’s nothing underneath it. Governance decides what you’re going to do, security protects it, and compliance proves you’re actually maintaining it — not just checking boxes. People lump those three together and assume “we’re compliant.” Well, when did you last check? Where the rubber meets the road is: am I maintaining this consistently, is there an audit trail, and if someone walked in today and asked you to prove your AI governance is being followed, could you hand them the evidence? Most aren’t ready to prove that compliance.

Chris Hutchins: The idea of controls that govern it is very different from a policy in a PDF. What we signed off on a month ago — the models keep evolving and training and moving. How do we deal with that?

Aaron Puckett: Because AI moves in milliseconds, you have to have AI tools and other software that monitor what AI is doing. Take the web browser: if I can log into ChatGPT or Claude, what’s tracking that? There are tools that will tell you, “wait, this is the free version, you haven’t logged in,” and stop you. You can set parameters that say you can’t upload PHI — that’s data loss prevention — and it will block an upload that contains recognizable Social Security numbers, financial information, or a medical record, and flag it as not permitted. For AI bolted onto other systems, the controls fall back to conditional access: am I allowed to log into a medical record from outside the United States? It’ll block me, even if I’m a doctor trying to log into Epic. And permission levels matter — whatever access you have as a user is what your AI has access to. People don’t understand that. If your AI doesn’t have guardrails, it has access to everything you do. If you don’t check what your employees can access, they’re exposed — and AI explodes that liability because it’s a super-intelligence without guardrails. So you deploy tools to monitor and allow or disallow what AI can access, and to create an audit trail. There are also tools that catch exploitation — “why is Aaron suddenly pulling up 200 medical records and trying to download them?” That’s an indicator of compromise; someone may have breached the account. Add business-email-compromise prevention, account-takeover protection, endpoint detection — the list goes on. But AI has to have limits and guardrails too, and it takes a tool to watch the tool — always with a human in the loop getting alerts, and that has to happen in seconds, not minutes or hours. A car is a great invention, but it can also cause crashes — that doesn’t make it bad by itself. It’s about who’s using it, and whether there are literally guardrails.

Chris Hutchins: Let’s talk ownership. I’ve seen people move from one role to another and their access only expands — we never remove what they no longer need. What we’re talking about now is a whole different order of magnitude. So who’s accountable — security, compliance, the board, or all of them?

Aaron Puckett: It starts at the top — the board and the executive leadership team — and it needs to involve legal. If they have a strong internal cybersecurity team, fine, but it’s no different from hiring a CPA firm to do an audit; often you bring in a third party to help. Either way, it starts and stays at the top — they’re ultimately responsible — and ongoing, it needs to be part of board meetings. Publicly traded companies now have CISOs, and if there’s a breach it has to be disclosed quickly; these are seriously detrimental events, intentional or not. Look at MGM losing hundreds of millions to a social-engineering attack on their help desk. Governance is great, but staying compliant with it is the hard part, because there are always new threat actors, staffing shortages, regulatory pressure, and cyber-insurance policies that change. You have aging infrastructure and cloud sprawl — everything keeps growing, but nobody goes back to check for configuration drift, cloud drift, or permission-level drift. So it comes down to identity security posture management: who are you, what do you have, and what’s your security posture? People get promoted, demoted, or shift to a sister company but still have access to the old system — and third parties are a risk too. Think about all the vendor integrations in a healthcare organization. Remember Target — it was the HVAC vendor that got hacked and then got into Target because of permission levels. This is constant; it’s someone’s full-time job to make sure all the doors are locked. But don’t over-restrict either — most people can’t actually work in a true zero-trust environment where everything requires permission. You don’t put a million-dollar lock on a five-thousand-dollar problem; you put the right tools in place while still being able to function.

Chris Hutchins: Let me make this concrete. There’s one scenario every operator has had a nightmare about: shadow AI. Katherine Tuominen was recently on the show and described frontline staff feeding patient data straight into public LLMs — not maliciously, just trying to work faster. From where you sit, how big is that really?

Aaron Puckett: It’s everywhere. For many organizations that was the red flag when they found it — but they don’t know the extent of it. I recently wrote a blog on this: AI is already in your organization; the question is who’s in control of it, and it’s not you. So beyond governance and policy, you need training. Security awareness training now has to include proper use of AI — what’s allowed to be uploaded — and then a tool that prevents the rest. People aren’t being malicious; they logged into a free LLM and uploaded something because they’re under pressure and their workload is piled up. They think they’re being helpful, they drop it into an LLM, and Pandora’s box opens.

Chris Hutchins: I don’t want to paint a horrible picture, but people need to understand this. When someone pastes PHI into a public chatbot, walk me through what actually happens to that data in plain terms.

Aaron Puckett: It’s now on the world wide web. Anyone with access to that model now potentially has access to that PHI, because you agreed to share it with the model. There are cases of people working on top-secret projects who put information into the model, and suddenly people asking a similar question are getting your IP or that patient’s PHI back, because you put it into a worldwide database. There won’t be HIPAA police slapping handcuffs on you, but there’s reputational damage, financial loss, a domino effect. What you can do is train people and put a tool in place that monitors and prevents it: “yes, you have access because we pay for ChatGPT, but it forces you to log into your account, notifies you that prompts are logged for audit, and prevents you from uploading PHI.” Or you blanket-block it. But I’ve heard of people taking a picture of a record on their phone and uploading it — now it’s on their phone, which is itself a violation. So there’s a lot of training involved, not just restricting everything, but training people on proper usage and putting the guardrails in place.

Chris Hutchins: It’s not crystal clear for people — “we sent a policy email, so we’re good, right?” Let’s talk about the balance of technical controls and culture, because I don’t think this is just a tooling problem or just a training problem.

Aaron Puckett: It’s all of the above. People are used to oversharing — I learned long ago not to post my kids’ ages or “Timmy’s starting first grade at this school, here’s our dog Rover,” because that can be used to breach my accounts. Your open-source intelligence — OSINT — is already out there. Humans naturally overshare. The challenge is that I’m responsible for me, but an organization has hundreds or thousands of people. So training has to come from the top, and leadership has to support it. If you’re going to unleash this tool, you have to teach people how to use it — you don’t hand a kid a chainsaw without showing them how. Ongoing training, ongoing reminders, ongoing monitoring and support, and constantly evaluating the tools you deploy. If you have a CISO or CTO or a team that rolls these things out and tests them first — a security-first posture — that’s key, because any exposure you have, that tool will make a thousand times worse.

Chris Hutchins: Every team you work with is already stretched. When you walk in and say “we need to get you audit-ready for AI on top of SOC 2 and HIPAA,” how do you do that without it becoming an unfunded mandate that burns the team out?

Aaron Puckett: It does need to be funded — going for SOC 2 will expose a lot. So we start with an assessment for whatever compliance level it is — SOC 2, PCI, HIPAA, NIST — identify the gaps, and build a roadmap to address them. Getting compliant won’t happen tomorrow; look at CMMC taking organizations six months to a year before the audit even comes. It’s about getting audit-ready and mapping what’s needed to meet those levels — sometimes it’s just meeting the CIS minimum standard, sometimes it’s a full SOC 2 Type 1 one-time audit and then ongoing proof of a strong posture. You need a compliance champion inside the organization — one or two individuals who own this — working with a team that helps them get assessment-ready.

Chris Hutchins: What’s the highest-leverage thing a strained team could do first — the 20% that gets 80% of the protection?

Aaron Puckett: The first line of defense is multi-factor authentication, enforced across the board. That’s number one.

Chris Hutchins: Could you explain what that means? Not everyone understands it.

Aaron Puckett: Multi-factor authentication — you’ve heard of 2FA. When you log into your computer or your electronic health record that has an AI tool embedded, it’s authenticating that you are who you say you are: my phone is pre-set, I get a prompt, I see it’s me, and I approve it — or I enter a code, or I get an email. Sometimes it’s passkeys like facial recognition or Windows Hello, which I use because it’s quicker. So beyond your password, it uses a second or third factor. There’s Microsoft Authenticator, Google’s authenticator, Duo from Cisco — they have different layers. And you can steal MFA too — session jacking, token theft — so good systems add “impossible travel”: if I log in from Orlando and then a few hours later from Tokyo, it knows someone else has my password. It checks the device — “Aaron has an iPhone on this iOS; this login is an old iPhone or an Android with a different MAC address.” So it layers device awareness on top. Beyond MFA there’s DNS filtering and email phishing defense — now embedded with AI — that can see, “wait, Aaron shouldn’t be sending 200 emails at 2 a.m.,” or “we’re stopping this email because it contains PHI, a bank account, or a credit card number.” So the AI stops me, or stops someone who’s taken over my account in a business-email-compromise, and notifies someone to remediate. At this point it’s AI versus AI.

Chris Hutchins: How should an operator think about the AI features their existing vendors are quietly turning on inside tools they already trust?

Aaron Puckett: Same as any new tool: evaluate before you grant permission, and train on it before it’s deployed. Any exposure you have will only be amplified once that tool is on. Sometimes a vendor bolts AI onto a tool you already have — was that a rush to market? Was there DevSecOps when they built it? Was security considered? Do your due diligence even with a well-known EHR — get the documentation, get the SOC 2 Type 2, read what it has access to. Everything’s over-permissioned now, so the second you sign on with single sign-on and MFA, that tool may have access to everything — all the APIs — and it becomes a domino. Treat it just like any new tool, because it is new — verify it before you roll it out.

Chris Hutchins: We get too comfortable. Systems can’t be managed the way they used to — “we’ll have an upgrade in Q3” — things change much faster than that.

Aaron Puckett: I’ve gotten the Change Healthcare breach letter, and one from a vendor whose access went back to records from my prior insurer in the mid-2000s — “here’s three years of credit monitoring.” This happens to major organizations, healthcare or not, even billion-dollar companies with huge security and development teams. So how much more exposed is someone under pressure, doing more with less, dealing with financial constraints or the day-to-day of healthcare, where this falls to the background? It can have just as detrimental an effect.

Chris Hutchins: I want to give you room here — you’ve published a point of view. Talk to us about what’s behind it.

Aaron Puckett: The governance risk is lawsuits, HIPAA violations, medical malpractice. In AI radiology — looking for lymph nodes, lung nodules, tumors — there’s liability if there isn’t proper procedure, governance, and someone overseeing that you’re maintaining compliance. It’s not set-it-and-forget-it. “We deployed AI a year ago” — but no one’s reviewed the drift or the configuration since. It exposes huge risk, even internally with vendor or banking fraud — there’s a lot of money moving around, and if AI is in the mix, it can be leveraged by bad actors. Everything is in the computers now; everything we do is accessible from almost anywhere. People used to think about the perimeter — protect the castle with a moat and walls and you’re fine inside. No — you have to assume it’s not if but when they get through, and ask what damage can be done once they’re inside. AI exposes all of that if you don’t have the tools, policies, and procedures in place. You have to be right every single time; they only have to be right once. The exposure in healthcare is some of the largest out there — the most sensitive information, the most sensitive population, huge amounts of financial data, endless claims and denials and prescriptions going back and forth. It’s a dedicated full-time job for a team to protect the financial well-being of the organization. Dump 25,000 patient records and now you’ve got a firm like Morgan & Morgan running a class action that costs tens of millions — on top of reputational damage and rebuilding the infrastructure while you’re fighting the lawsuit. Google it; these are happening all the time. You don’t want to be on that side of a news report.

Chris Hutchins: Did you ever see the digital glitter-bomb videos on YouTube? If you could design a digital one, that would be cool.

Aaron Puckett: I haven’t — I’ll have to look that up. I’ve seen the porch-pirate ones where someone steals an Amazon package and they trace it. A digital version would be great. That’s where digital forensics and incident response come in — hopefully you have someone on retainer; that’s not our specialty. A cyber incident is like an explosion, and what happens after is much messier and more complicated. Our job is to prevent it in the first place and then minimize the damage by stopping it as quickly as possible — we’re 24/7/365. Because attackers are using their own AI, or exploiting a company’s AI, these attacks happen far faster than before — seconds instead of minutes, minutes instead of hours — so they can do more sophisticated damage and cover their tracks on the way out with your data. Everybody thinks it’s just ransomware; it’s not. Ransomware is one weapon. It’s everything that can do financial harm — bad state actors, financial gain, anarchists — and on top of that you have plain human mistakes, like someone dumping PHI into an LLM. The human is an attack surface, even just accidental data loss.

Chris Hutchins: I want to land this somewhere useful for someone running an organization. If I’m a healthcare operator who’s been nodding along but has no idea where to start, what’s the first move I make this week?

Aaron Puckett: Ask yourself: where is my sensitive data in the organization? That’s the first question. If you don’t know the answer, that’s a huge problem — you’ve just identified that you need help. Then reach out to a technology, cybersecurity, and compliance consultant, because if you don’t know where your PHI and sensitive data live, that’s the canary in the coal mine before you do anything. And then: how are you protecting it? Whether it’s employee records, patient records, or financial records, it’s all extremely sensitive. If you don’t know where it lives or who has access, don’t do anything — don’t deploy any tools. Stop in your tracks, get those questions answered, and get control of your data before you deploy anything. Why does it matter? Because it’s a matter of being in business or not.

Chris Hutchins: These are things that need to be understood before you take an AI initiative to the board for approval. Silence or blank stares when you ask your teams — that should alarm everybody.

Aaron Puckett: And don’t assume your IT team has it handled — that’s the misconception. It needs to be owned and understood at the board and executive level, not by the IT person who’s busy running across campus to fix a printer. They’re caught in the weeds, and it’s not their responsibility. Think of them as your primary care physician — a hospital doesn’t just have primary care; it has every specialty that requires extra degrees, fellowships, and expertise. That’s the level of complexity we’re talking about. It’s not a single person; it’s a collection of people, tools, and skill sets to accomplish it and keep everything protected.

Chris Hutchins: Aaron, this has been a very educational conversation. Before we wrap, where can people find you and Managed Services Group if they want to reach out?

Aaron Puckett: You can visit our website — msgrouponline.com, or msgroupsecure.com, another of our domains — and there’s a quick form to fill out. Or find me on LinkedIn; we’re all very active there, so follow me and follow our page. I’ve never done a podcast, so thank you for having me on, Chris — it’s been a nice experience.

Chris Hutchins: It’s been a pleasure. Listeners, take a look at the show notes — we put all the information there so you know how to reach Aaron and the team. The takeaway I’m sitting with is that the policy is the easy part; the question is whether there’s anything underneath it.

Aaron Puckett: Yep — and can you prove it?

Chris Hutchins: Exactly. Thanks again for coming on The Signal Room, Aaron — it’s been an absolute pleasure. I’m Chris Hutchins, and we’ll see you next time on The Signal Room.

Aaron Puckett: Thank you again.

Why Patient Care Is Suffering (And How to Fix Your Workflow)

with Ted Lindsley · CEO, CognomIQ

Recorded on location in Dallas, a conversation about why healthcare data integration keeps breaking down, and what leaders should demand from the systems and vendors responsible for fixing it.

Show Notes

Ted Lindsley spent three decades working outside healthcare before deciding to enter it. As CEO of CognomIQ, he now works on the data layer underneath health systems — the integration, architecture, and vendor arrangements that determine whether information reaches a clinician in time to matter. Recorded on location in Dallas, this conversation examines why healthcare keeps investing in data platforms without getting trusted information out of them, and what leaders should require from the vendors responsible for fixing it.

What We Cover

  • Why he spent 30 years avoiding healthcare before entering it
  • The payroll test for identifying a broken business process
  • EHR constraints, bolt-on tools, and vendor lock-in
  • How organizations become accustomed to a broken system
  • Buying results instead of hopes and promises
  • Guarantees, architecture, and vendor sprawl
  • What happens the first time a clinician touches a tool
  • The standard for near-real-time healthcare data
  • Why proofs of concept do not become operational capabilities
  • Accountability when technology fails to deliver
  • Dashboard sprawl and the burden placed on physicians

Key Takeaways

  • Buy results, not promises. Lindsley argues that vendors should put the outcome in writing with an explicit consequence attached. His own contracts carry a one-year, 100% money-back guarantee.
  • The budget usually already contains the money. When leaders say there is nothing in the budget, his response is that there is too much in it — the spend is going to vendor sprawl and manual work.
  • Total cost of ownership misses the intangibles. He describes a data-platform contract where 30 offshore contractors crunching data by hand cost roughly $2.5M a year as a separate line item, never attached to the platform in the cost conversation.
  • Speed changes what an organization can attempt. He cites ad hoc reports moving from about three months to three hours and 24 minutes, and grant proposals from a quarter to about a week.
  • Accountability is the missing ingredient. Integration does not usually fail because the technology is impossible. It fails when no one is contractually on the hook for the outcome.
